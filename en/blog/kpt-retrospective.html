KPT Retrospectives: A 30-Minute Wrap-Up After Any Project

2026-11-14 · 5 min read

When a project ends, everyone is tired and moves straight on to the next thing — and then repeats the same mistakes in the next project. A retrospective doesn’t need to be elaborate. Three areas and thirty minutes are enough.

What is KPT?

  • Keep — what went well and what we will keep doing
  • Problem — what was disappointing or slowed us down
  • Try — what we will try next time

It is simple, but the order matters. Starting with what went well relaxes the room and makes people less defensive when problems come up.

Prepare the canvas (2 min)

Draw three rectangles side by side and label them Keep, Problem and Try. It helps to give each area its own note color: green for Keep, pink for Problem, yellow for Try. Write the scope above the areas — for example “This project: XX redesign (Sep–Nov)” — so the conversation doesn’t wander.

Write Keeps (5 min)

Everyone quietly adds notes to the Keep area. The more specific the better: “The ten-minute check-in every morning was great.” When everyone is done, each person reads theirs briefly, and similar notes are stacked together.

Write Problems (8 min)

This is the step that needs the most care. There is one rule: write about situations, not people. Instead of “X handed things over late”, write “Design review overlapped with the development schedule.” Separating the problem from the person is what makes it possible to talk about solutions.

If many Problems come up, group similar ones and use dot voting to pick the two or three that hurt most.

Problems that are hard to say out loud are easier to write on a note. Give people plenty of quiet writing time.

Decide on Tries (10 min)

Attach one Try to each Problem you voted for. A Try must be small enough to start next week. “Let’s communicate better” is a resolution, not a Try. “Fix design review to Tuesday afternoons every week” is a Try.

Wrap up (5 min)

Move the Tries into calendar to-dos and assign owners. Then schedule the next retrospective right away. It begins by checking whether last time’s Tries actually worked. Continuing like this turns the retrospective from a one-off event into a mechanism that helps the team improve little by little.

Save the board as a PNG and attach it to your project documents; six months from now, when you start a similar project, it will be a valuable reference.
